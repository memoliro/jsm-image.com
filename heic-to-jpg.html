What HEIC is and why your iPhone uses it

HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container) is Apple's implementation of the HEIF image standard. Instead of the decades-old JPEG compression, it uses HEVC — the same modern compression behind 4K video — to store photos at roughly half the file size of JPG at equal or better quality. Since iOS 11, iPhones save photos as HEIC by default, which is why a phone with limited storage can still hold thousands of pictures.

The format itself is excellent. The problem is compatibility. macOS and iOS handle HEIC natively, but Windows needs an extra codec from the Microsoft Store, many Android apps still choke on it, and plenty of websites, email clients, and older programs simply refuse .heic uploads. That is the moment most people search for a HEIC to JPG converter: the photo is fine, but nothing else can open it.

What happens to quality during conversion

HEIC can store more than JPG — 10-bit color and HDR brightness data versus JPG's 8-bit standard range. When you convert HEIC to JPG, that extra data is mapped down to what JPG can hold. In practice, for normal photos viewed on normal screens, the difference is invisible: the converted JPG looks the same as the HEIC did on your phone.

Two things are worth knowing. First, JPG is lossy, so pick a high quality setting (85–92) for the conversion and avoid re-saving the JPG again later — every lossy save discards a little more detail. Second, the HEIC you start from is already compressed, so converting cannot add quality that was never there; it just preserves what exists in the most compatible package.

Should you keep the HEIC originals?

For photos you care about, yes — archive the originals. The JPG is for sharing, uploading, and compatibility; the HEIC is the higher-quality master. Storage is cheap, and you cannot reconstruct the original from a converted file. A simple workflow: convert copies for everyday use, and keep the HEIC files in a backup folder or cloud archive untouched.

The exception is casual snapshots you only need once — a receipt photo, a picture for a message. For those, converting and moving on is perfectly fine.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Converting a JPG that was already converted. Each HEIC → JPG → JPG round trip loses a little detail. Always convert from the original HEIC file, not from a previously converted copy.
  • Deleting originals before checking the results. Open a few converted JPGs first — zoom in, check faces and text — before you delete anything.
  • Uploading HEIC files directly to websites. Many upload forms accept the file and then fail silently, or show a broken preview. Convert to JPG first and save yourself the confusion.
  • Using very low quality to save space. Below ~75, skies and skin tones start showing banding. If the file is too big, resize the dimensions instead — it looks far better than crushing the quality.
  • Forgetting about Live Photos and bursts. A Live Photo is a HEIC plus a short video clip; converting gives you the still image only. If you need the motion, export the video separately from your phone.

Frequently asked questions

Is converting HEIC to JPG free?

Yes. Conversion runs entirely in your browser with no account, no upload, and no per-file limits — convert one photo or a hundred.

Will I lose image quality converting HEIC to JPG?

At quality 85–92, no visible loss. JPG holds less color data than HEIC on paper, but for normal photos the converted image looks identical to the original on screen.

Why won't Windows open my HEIC photos?

Windows does not ship with the HEIF codec by default — it is a separate download from the Microsoft Store, and even then some apps ignore it. Converting to JPG sidesteps the problem entirely: JPG opens everywhere with no extra software.

Can I convert HEIC to PNG or WebP instead of JPG?

Yes. PNG is best when you need lossless quality (though the files will be large); WebP is the best pick for website images, since it is much smaller than JPG at the same visual quality.

Are my photos uploaded to a server during conversion?

No. The HEIC decoding and JPG encoding happen locally in your browser, using code running on your device. Your photos never leave your computer — which is also why there is no waiting for uploads.
