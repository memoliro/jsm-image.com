How to Resize, Convert & Crop Images Online

JSM-Image.com runs entirely in your browser. Upload an image, set the size or preset, optionally crop and compress, then download — without sending your file to our servers.

Ready to edit?
Open the free image tool

1. Upload your image

Click Select Image(s), drag and drop, or paste (Ctrl/Cmd+V). Supported formats include JPG, PNG, WebP, AVIF, GIF, SVG, and HEIC/HEIF. You can select multiple files for batch work.

2. Choose dimensions or a preset

Enter width and height, or pick a social preset (Instagram, YouTube, TikTok, profile picture, and more). Keep aspect ratio locked unless you intentionally want to stretch the image.

3. Crop (optional)

Open the Crop tab, choose freeform or a ratio like 1:1 or 9:16, adjust the handles, then Apply Crop.

4. Convert format

Use Convert Image Format for JPG, PNG, WebP, or AVIF. WebP and AVIF often produce smaller files than JPG at similar visual quality. Animated GIFs export as a single frame.

5. Compress & download

On the Compression tab, lower quality for smaller files (JPG/WebP/AVIF). Preview the estimated size, then download one image or process a batch as a ZIP.

Understanding image size: pixels, dimensions, and aspect ratio

Every digital image is a grid of pixels, and its size is written as width × height — for example, 4000 × 3000 means four thousand pixels across and three thousand down, roughly 12 million pixels (12 megapixels). More pixels carry more detail, but they also make a bigger file.

The aspect ratio is the width-to-height relationship: 4:3, 3:2, 16:9, 1:1. Resize with exact dimensions when a platform demands a precise size (a 1920 × 1080 banner), or percentage scaling when you just need it smaller (50% before emailing).

Keep the aspect-ratio lock on unless you deliberately want to stretch the image. Typing arbitrary numbers with the lock off distorts faces and objects — the classic squished-photo look. If you need a different shape, crop first, then resize.

Which format should you convert to?

Format choice matters more than most people expect. A quick decision guide:

  • JPG — the universal photo format; it opens everywhere. Quality 80–85 is the sweet spot for web, email, and social media.
  • PNG — lossless, best for screenshots, logos, and graphics with text or sharp lines. Too bulky for camera photos.
  • WebP — typically 25–35% smaller than JPG at similar quality, supported by all modern browsers. The best default for website images.
  • AVIF — often the smallest of all, sometimes half the size of JPG. Local encoding is slower, so save it for hero images rather than quick shares.
  • HEIC — the iPhone camera default. Convert to JPG before sharing with non-Apple users.

Cropping: composition and common aspect ratios

Cropping recomposes the photo, not just trims edges. The rule of thirds is the simplest upgrade: imagine a 3×3 grid and place your subject along the lines or their intersections instead of dead center. Trim distractions and straighten horizons before resizing.

Common aspect ratios and where they are used:

  • 1:1 — profile pictures, Instagram posts
  • 4:5 — Instagram portrait posts
  • 16:9 — YouTube thumbnails and banners, widescreen slides
  • 9:16 — stories, reels, TikTok, phone wallpapers
  • 3:2 — classic camera ratio, prints

Crop before resizing: discarding wasted pixels first gives a sharper, smaller result.

Compression basics and realistic size targets

Two levers control file size: dimensions first, quality second. A photo shown at 1200 px but stored at 4000 px carries ten times the pixels it needs. Resize to the display size first, then compress — this beats aggressive quality settings and looks better.

Sensible targets to aim for:

  • Website hero image — 150–300 KB, WebP, around 1920 px wide
  • Blog and content images — 80–150 KB
  • Thumbnails — under 50 KB
  • Email attachments — under 500 KB each, ideally under 1 MB total
  • Social media uploads — under 1–2 MB; platforms recompress anyway, so don't obsess
  • Profile pictures — under 200 KB

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Enlarging a small image and expecting detail. Upscaling only invents blur — start from the largest original you have.
  • Unlocking the aspect ratio by accident. One stretched export ruins an otherwise good photo; keep the lock on.
  • Re-saving a JPG as JPG repeatedly. Every lossy save discards more detail. Always work from the highest-quality original.
  • Using JPG for screenshots and text. Artifacts cluster around sharp edges — exactly where text lives. Use PNG for anything with words.
  • Ignoring the final display size. A 6000 px image shown at 800 px wastes bandwidth for zero visual gain.

Frequently asked questions

Will resizing make my image quality worse?

Shrinking is safe — fewer pixels still look sharp at the new size. Enlarging is risky: stretching a small image across more pixels only produces blur. Always start from the largest original you have.

What is the best format for website images?

WebP is the best default today: 25–35% smaller than JPG at similar quality, with broad browser support. Use AVIF for maximum compression on hero images where encoding time doesn't matter, PNG for screenshots and graphics with text, and JPG when you need maximum compatibility.

Can I resize without cropping?

Yes. Resizing with the aspect-ratio lock on changes the dimensions while keeping the shape — nothing is cut off. Cropping is only needed when you want a different shape, like turning a landscape photo into a square profile picture.

Why does my converted image look worse than the original?

Usually one of two causes: the quality setting was too low, or a JPG was recompressed from an already-compressed copy. Convert from the original at quality 80–85 and compare before downloading.

Are my images uploaded to a server during editing?

No. Resizing, converting, cropping, and compression all run locally in your browser. Files are read from your disk into browser memory and written back as downloads — nothing is sent anywhere, which is also why batch jobs finish so quickly.
